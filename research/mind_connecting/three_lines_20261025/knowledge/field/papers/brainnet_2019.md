# BrainNet: a concrete boundary on the brain-bridge analogy

Jiang et al. (2019), *BrainNet: A Multi-Person Brain-to-Brain Interface for Direct Collaboration Between Brains*. [Primary PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-019-41895-7.pdf), DOI 10.1038/s41598-019-41895-7. Read: introduction, task/results, reliability analysis and discussion, pp. 1–7, Figures 1–3, 6–7; not a complete methods/supplement audit.

**Problem and comparison.** Can several people collaborate through a neural interface, and selectively rely on a better source? Five triads played a two-round block-rotation game. Two senders saw task information missing from the receiver's screen. EEG-decoded recommendations became occipital TMS signals; the receiver acted through EEG. Senders saw that action and could correct it. Software corrupted one sender's decisions. Thus this was a loop through task feedback, not reciprocal transplantation of rich cognitive states.

**Finding and limits.** Reported average accuracy was 81.25%; time-course analyses supported greater reliance on the more reliable sender. The trial count was small, with timestamp exclusions in one triad. The study establishes useful mediated communication and source-sensitive integration; it does not measure shared experience or loss of personal identity.

**Use here — our extension.** A brain-derived signal can remain treated as testimony from a distinguishable source. The interesting unknown for this project is therefore not whether an internal route inevitably erases boundaries. Ask whether recipient use, source reliability learning and source attribution separate when equivalent task information arrives through different computational routes. This requires fresh reliable communication and distinct manipulation of source history and present payload; old attribution effects alone cannot answer it. A negative result in which source discrimination remains accurate while access improves would also advance the original vision.
