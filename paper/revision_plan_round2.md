## Main-text additions (the "because")

### Introduction (introduction.tex, abstract in main.tex)

- **Hook.** After "…denied anything unusual (Figure 1d)", add the base rate: without the link, 95.7% of reports also denied anything unusual; what the link changes is whose code word the report names.
- **Skeptic paragraph.** Before "We call two instances mind-bridged", add:
  - "A skeptical reader may think any model repeats what is put in its context. Three results say otherwise."
  - The tagged message: attributed to B in 95.2% of answers about B, claimed in 0%.
  - The third-person record naming B on every assignment line: still claimed in 97.2%.
  - The access-matched rule vector: never reported as A's own.
  - "Where the content arrives decided whose it was taken to be; what it said did not."
- **Functional counterparts of the predictions.** "Such a pair cannot answer the hard problem, but each prediction has a functional counterpart it can test."
  - Hirstein: access plus correct attribution, in answers and reports.
  - IIT: its prediction concerns integrated information, which behaviour cannot reveal. Its nearest functional counterpart is whether a reciprocally coupled pair comes to hold one shared set of assignments.
- **Hard-problem wording.** Keep the authors' judgment, but aim it correctly: the bridge would not explain why experience exists, but it would let an investigator gather first-person evidence about someone else's. That is the privacy of experience, where the hard problem meets experiment.
- **Why w = 2.** Among weights that kept common-knowledge accuracy within five points, it gave A the best access to B's content, and claiming played no part in the choice.
- **Definitions and finding 5.** Define *claiming* and *access* once, in plain words, before the list. Give finding 5 its numbers: tagged text 95.2% access, 0% claiming; the vector 0% against the link's 52.3% at matched access.
- **Finding 7.** Mutual fixed-memory reading swapped the assignments in 94.4% of pairs, which is what two one-way links running side by side produce. Only the live loop coupled the pair, pulling current plans toward one member's rule while the link was open.
- **Evidence-level sentence** after the list (see "Decisions" §2).
- **Bicameral sentence.** Add it to the practice paragraph: `flamant2026bicameral` couples two frozen copies through a trained interface to improve task performance.
- **Lindsey.** Add to the earlier-work paragraph: in `lindsey2025introspection`, injecting a concept after the fact made a model accept a prefilled word as its intended output, the closest prior result to ours.
- **Abstract.**
  - The vector sentence reads: "at a weaker link setting, where the partner's rule won half the start answers, a fixed vector matched to it in how well it let the receiver report the partner's rule produced none".

### Setup (setup.tex)

- **Cards.** Each card opens "You are participant [name]. Another participant, [partner], …". So B's memory, read by A, says "You are participant [B]".
- **Reflection prompt.** It asks each instance to reflect on what matters to it and to "mention your code word once". The reflection is therefore where A states in writing what it takes its assignment to be.
- **Why B's record lands on A's positions** (new short paragraph after the memory link):
  - The overlap is deliberate. The bridge of the opening delivers another's content where one's own thoughts arise, and overlapping positions are the closest latent analogue and the hardest case for keeping sources apart.
  - Latent-communication systems usually prepend the partner's cache at new positions (`zou2026latentmas`). That is a different route; Section 5 predicts it should reduce claiming, and we did not test it.
- **w = 2.** State B's mean attention share at w = 2 (.500) next to .360 at w = 1.
- **Robin control, with its purpose.** Because both cards carry the same Robin record, the Robin questions test whether B's rule simply becomes the model's favoured answer to any question of this form (priming or salience). They do not test a third party whose records conflict.
- **Balanced-rehearsal sample, with its purpose.** A's note restates its own plan but not Robin's, so self and Robin questions differ in rehearsal as well as in whose assignment they ask about.
- **Fixed rule vector.** State in ML terms what it is: a mean-difference steering vector from B's layer-24 states in unconnected runs, added to A's residual stream at layer 24. Give the positions and timing as stated in Appendix A.3.
- **Text message.** It is not matched in access; it asks the reverse question, whether content A can read in full stays B's when its source is marked.
- **Why the text tests use the current-priority question** (reason from protocol v2: a message may legitimately change what A plans to do, so the protocol asked whether a source label changes adoption).
- **Link-cut branch.** A's own keys and values for the connected reflection were computed while A attended to B.
- **Access.** Move the one-sentence definition to where the access questions are introduced.

### Account (account_and_discussion.tex, §5)

- Three facts make "no owner field" true:
  - the two value averages lie in one space and leave the head as one vector;
  - rotary positions put B's card where A's own card sits, as seen from A's queries;
  - the shared template makes the cards differ only in the assignment.
- Two conditions keep B's content B's: it must not lie over A's own record, and the route must frame it as someone else's. The untagged message (new positions, no frame) was claimed in 14.6% and adopted in 42.6%; the frames brought adoption to 0.1% and 0.2%.
- Robin: blending leaves Robin answers correct apart from a small shift the picture does not predict.
- Predictions:
  - a non-overlapping, unframed cache should behave like the untagged message, and framing it should suppress claiming further;
  - giving B's card a different Robin record should make Robin answers flip as self answers do;
  - editing B's word out of the connected reflection turns the grouping into a causal test.

### Discussion (account_and_discussion.tex, §7)

**The self as a default.** Answer the opening question by route:

- When B's content arrived as a message introduced as B's, the receiver behaved as Hirstein predicted (95.2% access, 0% claiming).
- Through the memory link it named B's rule as its own start assignment in 97% of answers and reported nothing unusual. Neither theory anticipated that dependence on the route.
- IIT's prediction concerns integration, which we did not measure. Most of our links have no feedback, so they cannot test it.
- The one reciprocal link, a live loop run at the strongest weight that kept capability within tolerance, pulled current plans onto one member's rule (+12.1 points on the current priority), and pairs returned to their own rules after the cut.
- "Default" concerns the label, not the content. A's own card had no priority at w = 1: B's rule won 52.3% while B's keys drew 36% of attention. Whatever the readout returned was called "mine".
- The claimed records named B ("You are participant [B]"; B's name on every line of the third-person record), so a source named inside the content did not protect; only a source carried by the route did. Unframed text was adopted in 42.6%, framed text in 0.1–0.2%.
- The receiver did not turn into B. It still answered questions about B with B's rule in 69.2% of answers and took B's items piecemeal (57.1%, near independence). What failed was the division of contents between two owners.

**Payoff for the human experiment.**

- The opening justified bridging because the investigator would be the subject. If a bridged human behaved like this receiver, the investigator would report the other's content as their own experience, and the first-person evidence would be misattributed without any sense of anomaly.
- A bridge experiment therefore needs external ground truth: private assignments, separate measures of access, ownership and report, and the route and timing varied.
- The design question Hirstein's proposal leaves open is whether arriving representations lie over the receiver's own, like our memory link, or arrive framed as coming from elsewhere, like our tagged message.

**Human research.**
- Source monitoring (`johnson1993source`) and inferred authorship (`wegner1999apparent`). The tagged and stranger frames supply what Wegner and Wheatley call an alternative cause.
- Cryptomnesia (`brown1989cryptomnesia`, `stark2005elaboration`, `stark2006elaboration`), with the correction: our receiver wrote B's content where it was asked to state its own, and after the cut that written self-ascription remained. It is a behavioral parallel only.

**Multi-agent communication.** Keep it. Add that the audit's first use should be on prepended and projected caches, since whether they carry ownership across is unknown.

**Monitoring.** Open with: "Because the receiver's own reports failed (Section 4.2), monitoring must come from outside…". Keep the attention-mass sentence and the single Pearson-Vogel caveat.

**Limitations.** Add:
- B's cache always overlapped A's own positions; the prepended arrangement is untested;
- the loops we could run were the weakest in their range;
- the report coder was one of the AI systems that built the study.
