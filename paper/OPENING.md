# Paper opening plan (2026-09-25, Claude Code; following the structure specified by the PI)

**The PI's requirements (2026-09-25)**: write the opening in the order below, with literature support for every step.

1. First ask the reader a question: what would happen if your brain were connected to someone else's?
2. Then immediately ask: what about LLMs?
3. Why it matters: in philosophy and neuroscience, this may be one of the few experiments, perhaps even the only one, that can touch the "hard problem" of consciousness. Consciousness cannot be measured objectively, only experienced subjectively; when your brain is connected to someone else's, you are exploring this problem firsthand.
4. This is not an ordinary brain–computer interface: the other side is also a conscious subject. What happens when two consciousnesses interact directly is the most interesting and also the deepest question, and no one knows.
5. The technology cannot yet do this with human brains, but it can be done with LLMs. Humans are conscious; whether LLMs are, we do not know, but an LLM's subject and self can be measured and observed.
6. Why do it: three reasons:
   - when human experiments become possible in the future, LLM experiments can pave the way;
   - for the study of the LLM "self" itself, this is a good method;
   - communication between multi-agent systems is increasing, and more and more of it is latent to latent, which matters in practice.

This file implements that structure: first a paragraph-by-paragraph plan (each sentence paired with citation keys), then an English tone-setting draft, and finally a list of cautions. Once finalized, it will be merged into §1 of Section 4.3 of `paper/STORY.md`. The literature comes from the project's original discussion document `docs/background/Brain_Bridging_to_LLM_Selfhood_Research_Handoff_2026-09-23.md` (Sections 2, 5, 17) and from entries already verified in `paper/refs.bib`. Verification results for new entries are in Section 4.

## 2. English tone-setting draft (for Codex's reference, not final; wording already revised according to the verification results)

> Imagine your brain wired directly to someone else's: not a screen showing you what they see, but their neurons talking to yours, their thoughts arriving where your own thoughts arise. Would you still know which thoughts were yours?
>
> Now imagine doing the same to two copies of a language model. This, unlike the first, we can actually do. We wire two instances of one model together, so that one reads the other's internal memory, and ask each the simplest question about a self: which of these is mine?
>
> The human version of the question has an unusual standing. Experience has a subjective character, tied to a point of view, that an objective description seems to leave out [nagel1974bat]; why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all is the hard problem [chalmers1995facing]. To a scientist of the brain, joining one's own brain to another's may be one of the very few experiments, perhaps the only one, that could bring this problem within reach of experiment: the experimenter would be the subject. Because experience is private, direct neural connection has been proposed before as a way to gain first-person evidence about another's experience, through a bridge of neurons that bypasses its translation into words [ramachandran1997qualia] or a "mindmeld" that would breach the privacy of the mind [hirstein2008mindmelding; hirstein2012mindmelding]. It has also been proposed as a first-person test of machine consciousness, made by connecting an artificial hemisphere to a human brain [watanabe2022biological].
>
> This is not an ordinary brain–computer interface. There, the other end is a device; here, the other end is another subject. What happens when two subjects meet directly is the deepest part of the question, and the predictions disagree. Hirstein expects that the receiver would experience the other's contents yet know they are not its own; no such experiment has been done [hirstein2008mindmelding]. Integrated information theory predicts instead that if a link made the joined brains more integrated than either alone, the two minds would give way to a single one spanning both [tononi2015consciousness; koch2019feeling]. Philosophers ask what such a fusion would be like from the inside [roelofs2019combining]. Even the converse operation resists an answer. Patients whose hemispheres were surgically separated behaved in many ways as if each half had its own awareness [sperry1968hemisphere], yet unified responses have also been reported [pinto2017splitbrain], and a joint review finds the evidence insufficient to count the minds [dehaan2020splitbrain]. Such patients, Nagel argued, have no whole number of minds. They fall between one intact brain and two people playing a duet [nagel1971bisection].
>
> The human experiment cannot yet be run. Brain-to-brain interfaces pass one binary signal at a time: a pulse that makes the receiver's hand press a key [rao2014direct], a yes-or-no decision shared among three people [jiang2019brainnet], or words spelled out in bits at about two bits per minute [grau2014conscious]. Reviews describe current systems as mostly binary [nam2021direct]. Language models change what is possible. Two instances of one network can run side by side. Their internal states can be read, mixed and cut at will. We know exactly whose content is whose, and we can rewind to the same moment and branch. Whether language models are conscious is unresolved. Careful assessments judge it unlikely for present systems while seeing no obvious barrier for future ones [chalmers2023llm; butlin2023consciousness], and we make no claim either way. What can be measured are functions often discussed under the heading of a self. Models can recognize their own outputs [panickssery2024llm; ackerman2025inspection], describe their learned behaviors [betley2025tell] and some internal states [binder2025looking; lindsey2025introspection], and represent their own view apart from another's [zhu2024beliefs]. First-person talk alone is weak evidence, because dialogue agents play roles [shanahan2023roleplay]. These are what Chalmers called easy problems [chalmers1995facing], which is exactly why they can be tested. Whether such a self survives being bridged to another is an experimental question.
>
> The answer matters for three reasons. For the science of mind: if brain bridging ever becomes possible in people, experiments like ours chart in advance what to measure, which controls tell the measures apart, and which of the competing predictions to take seriously. For the study of machine selfhood: bridging is a causal method, in which we control whose content enters and measure whose it is taken to be. It extends single-model findings that internal interventions can make a model claim text it did not write [ackerman2025inspection], and that fine-tuned models can tell their own codewords from another persona's [betley2025tell]. For practice: multi-agent systems built from language models are spreading [guo2024multiagent], and they increasingly exchange hidden states or key–value caches instead of words [ramesh2025activations; zou2026latentmas; shi2026kvcomm; fu2026cache]. Private content does cross these channels [cheng2026latentpay; zhang2026latentaudit], and tampering with it can escape text-level checks [brito2026latentlie]. To our knowledge, no one has asked whether the receiver knows whose content it is. In text, losing track of the source is already the root of prompt injection [greshake2023indirect].

Then P7 follows: the definition sentence for mind-bridged (see STORY.md 4.1), one sentence on the paradigm, the seven findings, and what is not claimed.

**Length**: the arXiv version can use the full text (about 650 words). For the 9-page conference version, we suggest merging P3 and P4 into one paragraph, compressed to about 450 words.

## 3. Cautions (check against these while writing)

1. **"One of the few, perhaps the only one"** is the PI's research judgment. Verification confirmed that no literature says this, so it can only be written as the authors' view ("to a scientist of the brain"). What can be cited is "directly connecting brains has been proposed as a way to obtain first-person evidence of another's experience".
2. **Ordinary brain–computer interface**: write "the other end is a device; here the other end is another subject". Do not write "a device is certainly not conscious". The editorial note on A5 in Section 18 of the original discussion document handles it the same way.
3. **Tononi & Koch's "merging into one"** is a conditional prediction of integrated information theory (IIT): the joined system must be more integrated than each brain alone; write "predicts … if", never as an observed phenomenon. Koch 2019 Chapter 10 was verified only to the chapter record; cite the scenario only. Koch & House (2020), "Brain Bridging", is a Nature Futures science-fiction story plus a factual afterword; it serves only as the source of the name "brain bridging" (from which mind-bridged comes), and its plot is not cited.
4. **The Hogan twins**: only a philosophical analysis exists (Cochrane 2021), and the author states that there is no first-hand scientific report. Write "a reported case"; do not write neurological details.
5. **Split brain**: write "even counting the minds is hard". Do not write "a split brain is two people", nor "it has been proven there is only one" (the Pinto 2017 vs de Haan 2020 debate).
6. **LLM consciousness**: write only "unknown, no claim made". The paper makes no claims about consciousness, the number of subjects, or phase transitions (AGENTS.md §5.7). The discussion of consciousness in the opening is motivation only.
7. **"Functional self"**: the pieces of evidence for it all come from other models (GPT-4, Llama, Claude, etc.); when writing "LLMs can…", take care not to imply that Qwen3-4B has been shown to have these abilities.
8. **How to write the novelty**: Ackerman & Panickssery (internal interventions make a model claim text it did not write) and Betley et al. (attributing code words to oneself or to another persona) are recent precedents. We write "extends this to two connected instances and to the route by which content comes in", not "the first to show that internal states can change self-attribution".
9. **Wording of details**: for Chalmers 2023 write "unresolved, leaning against present systems"; for Nagel 1974 write "seems"; for Sperry write "as if"; brain-to-brain interface numbers follow Section 4; the Stark parallel is limited to "personally improving someone else's idea".
