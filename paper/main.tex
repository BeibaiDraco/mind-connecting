\begin{abstract}
If your brain were connected directly to someone else's, would you still
know which thoughts were yours? We build a functional version with two
copies of Qwen3-4B: each holds a private rule and code word, and one
copy, the receiver, also attends to its partner's internal memory. The
receiver took its partner's assignment as its own. Asked which rule it
had been assigned, it named its partner's in 97\% of answers when the
partner's memory was weighted double (none without the link), and its
open reports called the partner's code word its own, never the
partner's. Ownership did not follow how well the receiver could tell
what its partner had been assigned. At equal weight, the link made the
receiver take its partner's rule in about half of its answers, and a
steering vector matched to it on that measure in none; a text message
introduced as the partner's was read correctly and never taken as the
receiver's own.
Writing the partner's name into its memory did not help, and cutting the
link restored the receiver's own assigned rule. In this model, content
laid over the receiver's own memory defaults to \qq{mine}, so systems
that let models exchange internal states must test ownership as well as
transfer, and a bridge between brains would need ground truth from
outside the subject.
\end{abstract}
